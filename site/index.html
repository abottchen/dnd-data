The Roots Fall Silent

Session XXXIII 4 Nightal 08 NOV 2026

There is a kind of villain who announces himself, and I have always been grateful to the type. A voice out of the dark that names itself the guardian of the thing you came for has told you three useful things: that the thing is worth guarding, that it is guarded, and that its keeper would sooner talk than strike first. The company took note of all three. Then Chumble Crudluck proposed a plan.

I give the plan in full, because a good deal of what follows hangs on it. The goblin would send Wickles, unseen, across the chamber to the great root. The pickle would take the coin from its hollow. The pickle would then eat the coin, be transformed by the power inside it into a thing the goblin called the "Dill Titan," lay waste to the Forsaken Tree from within, and carry the company out on its shoulders. Nobody argued for this plan. Nobody quite forbade it either. The feeling among them, as it was put to me, was that it would not be so simple, but that if by some miracle it worked they would go along with it.

The chamber, now that they had to fight in it, showed itself more plainly. The great root stood at the center with the coin in its hollow. Around it, well out toward the walls, stood four lesser roots, each as thick as a man is tall, running from floor to ceiling like the pillars of a hall. It was up these that the black energy climbed before it bent across the roof and poured down into the heart. The voice had come from behind the great root, on the side farthest from the door. Between the door and the root stood the dead.

There proved to be four of them, and they lasted about as long as it takes to say so. Lilac Mist's first arrow went wide, which she did not enjoy, and her second took the nearest so squarely that it came apart and stayed apart. The dead of Chult have an ugly habit of rising from blows that ought to finish them. None of these managed it. Mesika crossed half the chamber before anyone else had taken three steps, and the khopeshes put down the second. Chumble's blasts dropped the third, and as it fell something pale tore loose from the corpse and went streaking away to the south and west, through root and earth as though neither were there. The goblin, whose rod has its own claim on the souls of what he kills, watched it go with the look of a man who has had his pocket picked. Vex came in on the last with a scimitar in each hand and left a pile of bones.

Somewhere in the middle of this the pickle had set out.

Wickles crossed the breadth of the chamber unseen, as fast as a pickle can be made to go, and came to the hollow in the great root, and reached in. What happened next the company knows only through the goblin, who hears his familiar inside his own head. The report began well. He had it. He had it. He very nearly had it. And then: "Chumble, the roots. They are wrapped all the way around me. I cannot move. Oh, that is bad. That is the opposite of pickling. Everything is going grey and quiet, and Chumble, it is really cold in here."

There was a burst of the black energy at the mouth of the hollow, and there was no more pickle.

I ask my gentler readers not to grieve. A warlock's familiar is called back with an hour's work and a pinch of incense, and Wickles has died in these pages before. I do note, for the record, that this is not the first time something with tendrils has had hold of him, and that he is said to be forming opinions on the subject.

So much for the plan. What it had bought them was knowledge. The heart did not wish to be taken, and the roots would kill to keep it.

The guardian came out then, since nobody had come to him. He stepped from behind the great root, a man, or what a hundred years beneath that tree had left of one, and he did not waste the moment on a second speech. He threw his hand out at Mesika, the nearest of them, and the same black energy that ran in the walls went into her like water into sand, and for an instant the woman from Mezro looked as a plant looks in a drought. She set her teeth and kept her feet. He was back behind the root before anyone could answer him.

This was Kolate. A century ago he came over the sea, a wizard of Nimbral by the old accounts, and talked the high priestess Rhoga into showing him where the Heart of the Wild was kept, and stole it, and fled into the jungle with the Obanashi on his heels. They caught him on this ground. The tree is what grew of it. He named himself the heart's guardian. Whether he kept it or it kept him I could not tell you, and I am not certain he could have told you either.

Shago went after him the only way Shago goes after anything, at a dead run and shouting his own name, which is his battle cry and, I am assured, a good one. Urida had a quicker way to reach a man behind a root. She could not see him. She knew where the voice had come from, and she had the others' pointing arms, and a ball of fire does not need to be told exactly. It burst on the far side of the chamber and the company heard Kolate scream. Shago, who was nearest, saw the blood-colored sap on one of the pillars hiss and boil away under the heat, though the wood beneath never caught. And Grieg, who was watching the pillars because he had run out of zombies to watch, saw the black current in them run thicker for a moment, as though something had drawn hard upon it.

It was Vex who put steel to one first. Somebody had to. He set about the nearest pillar with both scimitars and opened it nearly halfway through in two strokes, and saw the same thing under his own blades: the more he cut, the more of the dark came flooding into the wood. Lilac Mist, from the mouth of the chamber, put two arrows into a second pillar. They stuck there and quivered, for a root minds an arrow a great deal less than it minds an edge, but that pillar darkened too, and the one Vex had been cutting did not.

Here I must give the wulven his due. Grieg is a hunter of beasts, not a scholar of the Art, and he will tell you so himself. But he stood there in the red light with his trident and thought it through, which is more than many a scholar would have done with a necromancer in the room. A pillar drank deeper when it was wounded. All of them drank deeper when Kolate was wounded. And a vessel that is filled and then broken must spill. So the pillars had to come down, since it was plainly through them that the heart was feeding its keeper, and whoever brought one down would be standing in whatever came out of it.

The company settled it in a few shouted words. Cut them nearly through. Then step back, and let the last blow fall from a distance.

Mesika opened the second pillar with three strokes and sprang clear of it. Chumble Crudluck, who had been standing very still since the hollow went quiet, found his voice. "You have hurt my pickle for the last time!" he told the chamber at large, and four rays of fire went from his hand into the farthest pillar of all, across the whole width of the dark, and every one of them struck. Then he folded himself into the roots as only a goblin can, and the chamber lost track of him entirely.

Grieg sent his echo to the pillar Mesika had opened. He put into its hand the short sword he has carried on his hip through this whole chronicle and had never once, by his own admission, drawn in anger. The echo chopped. It chopped again, and the root parted. The lower half slumped to the floor. Down the upper half the black energy came screaming, and burst, a blast no wider than a man's reach, and there was nothing within its reach but a wulven's shadow, which went out like a candle.

"Exactly as intended," said Grieg.

Kolate's answer came at once. He stepped out again, and a sickly yellow-green fog rolled out of his hands and settled over Vex, Dragonbait and Shago where they stood together by the pillar Vex had cut. Urida knew it on sight. She has never worked the spell, but every wizard has heard of the cloud that kills, and she called its name across the chamber, for whatever good a name could do them.

What did them good was the saurial. I have mentioned Dragonbait's sword in these pages and never yet had cause to describe it at work. When it is drawn, those who stand close to him bear sorcery better than they otherwise would. It is a grace of his calling. All three were standing close to him. They choked, and it burned in them, and they kept their feet.

Shago could not see his own spear. He knew which way the necromancer had gone, so he went that way, by feel, around the curve of the great root and out the far edge of the fog, and found Kolate standing directly in front of him. I am told both were surprised, and that Shago got over it first. The spear went into the wizard's shoulder to the binding.

And the tree answered.

Roots came down out of the ceiling over Kolate, reaching, as a mother snatches up a child from the road. Then they whipped back, and everything the three standing pillars had been drinking let go at once. It ran through every root in that chamber, and for all anyone can say through every root beneath the tree, and it went through the company like cold water through cloth. There was nowhere to stand that was out of it. Some of them shrugged off the worst. Vex did not, nor Dragonbait. And Urida, who is the smallest of them and wears nothing heavier than a robe, was left on her feet and very little more.

It had come through three pillars. It would have come through four if the echo had not cut one, and nobody needed the gnome to do that sum for them.

So they broke the rest, and quickly, while the roots were still spent.

Urida went first, though she had the least left to spend. Lilac Mist was calling to her to come back and be mended. The gnome's reply was to walk forward until she could see the farthest pillar, the one Chumble had scorched, and break the air around it with a clap of thunder that shook dust from the ceiling. Only then did she permit herself to be fussed over. The tabaxi came out to her at a run. The tail swished, the blue and violet sparks went round, and a good part of what the tree had taken from the gnome was given back.

Dragonbait felt his way out of the fog with one hand on the pillar Vex had been cutting, and when he could see it he took the holy sword in both fists and hit it. Light ran down the blade as it bit. It was the first time the company had seen that sword land on anything, and it took as much out of the root in a single stroke as the dragonborn's two scimitars had together. I am told there was a moment's respectful silence. He had a second stroke in him, and he held it, because Vex was standing at his elbow and the pillar was a hair from breaking. He stepped back instead.

Vex stepped back with him, well out of reach of it, and took a breath, and steadied himself, for the fog and the roots between them had cost him dearly. Then he breathed out.

My readers will recall the little gout of flame with which he closed his recital for the ghost Aviline, and which I called cosmetic. This one was not cosmetic. It took the pillar at its wounded middle and a man's height of root simply went to ash. The two ends dropped away from each other, and the black energy burst in the empty air between them and touched no one. Then the dragonborn walked out of the fog, unhurried, and took his place again at the saurial's side.

That was two. The third was the far one, scorched and thunder-struck and standing alone in the dark where only a goblin's eyes could find it. Chumble found it. One blast went wide. The second struck it square, and the pillar flew to splinters and spent its fury on an empty floor, unwitnessed, like the tree that falls in the forest in the old riddle.

That left one, the pillar on Kolate's own side of the chamber that had weathered the fireball. Grieg wanted it badly, and it was a long way round. He took the shortest line there was, close along the wall, where the lesser roots caught at his legs and tried to keep him, and he tore through them by main strength. He set his echo at the foot of the pillar and the short sword went to work again. It bit, and glanced, and Grieg dug for the burst of speed a fighter keeps in reserve for such moments, and it bit again and went through. The fourth pillar burst. The wulven lost a second shadow in as many minutes and did not appear to miss it.

With that the whole dark circuit that had so impressed the company from the doorway was broken. Nothing climbed the walls any more. Nothing poured into the heart. And there stood Kolate on the far side of the great root, with one of Shago's spears in him and nothing left at his back.

He did what he could. He turned the withering on Shago, and the guide set his jaw and took a small part of what was meant for him. Then it was Shago's turn. Kolate was quick: a shimmer of force sprang up on his arm and turned the first spear, and the second after it. It did not turn the shield. Shago put the rim of it into him with all his weight behind and laid him flat on his back among the roots, and across the chamber the yellow fog, with nobody left to mind it, thinned and went.

Urida came up on the other side and poured a cone of cold white light down the length of him, starlight by the look of it. It caught Shago's side as well, for he was standing over the man, and she called out that she was sorry, and Shago did not seem to notice he had been apologized to. Kolate noticed the light. When it faded he could see nothing farther off than the reach of a spear. And about his hands, where there had been nothing before, wisps of the black energy had begun to gather and turn.

They came at him from every quarter then. Mesika cut him once. Grieg stabbed down at him where he lay. Dragonbait was pounding around the root with the sword up. With every wound the darkness at his hands grew thicker.

I must leave them there a moment, because honesty requires me to report what Chumble Crudluck was doing while this went on.

He was at the coin. With the pillars down he had gone to the hollow in the great root to see what had killed his pickle, and found the heart shut in a little cage of rootlets, woven tight as a fist. So he took a flask of oil from his pack and emptied it over the cage, and struck a light from his tinderbox, and set it burning. The rootlets burned. They also grew, as fast as the fire could eat them, new wood pushing out through the flames. The goblin blew on it. That not sufficing, he turned his back on it and shook out the cloak the captain of the Horizon gave this company on their very first voyage, which billows grandly on command and had never until that hour been asked to do anything useful, and he fanned the fire with it. The fire roared. The roots grew. I am told it was a draw.

Meanwhile Kolate, flat on his back and all but blind, spoke a word and was not there.

Swords came down on bare roots. For a moment nobody knew where he had gone, save the two who could see the other side of the chamber. But a wulven's ears are not a man's. Grieg heard him land: a scuffle, and a weight coming to its feet against the far wall, beyond the great root. He shouted it out, and the company turned like a pack on a new scent.

Shago was off before the shout had finished, into a dark he could not see through, because that is the manner of the man. His road took him around the heart, past a goblin with his back to a fire, flapping a cloak. Chumble put out a hand for a slap of the palm as he went by. I cannot tell you whether he got one. I can tell you that Shago would have tried.

Urida's thunder found Kolate first, and he bore it. Then Lilac Mist had him. The fight had come round to her side of the chamber at last, and she laid her mark on him and loosed twice. The first arrow missed. The second he raised his shimmer against, and it went through that and through him as though neither were there. The darkness around his hands was by now blacker than anything else in that black place.

And Mesika, who had come down into the Forsaken Tree with a khopesh on each hip and no bow, and who found herself fifty feet from the enemy of her new friends with nothing to throw, did the thing a soldier does. She picked up rocks. The first two went nowhere near him. The third hit him square on the top of the head.

Chumble let the fire alone long enough to throw a larger one. It was the last great spell he had in him that day, and Kolate twisted away from the worst of it, and came out of the flames scorched and staggering and still gathering the dark to himself.

Every eye in the company could see what was building in his hands. None of them knew its name. They all knew they had come running to one place to kill him, and were now standing shoulder to shoulder around a man with one great spell left.

Grieg ran. He covered half the distance on his own legs and threw his echo the rest, and from where it stood over Kolate he struck through it with both hands on the trident. The first thrust went home. The necromancer sat up out of the roots with his hands coming together, the black energy pouring between them into a shape that was round and wide and meant for all of them. The second thrust went through both hands at once and into what lay behind them.

Urida never recognized the spell. I have described the gathering of it to others who know more of such workings than I do, and they went quiet, and asked how close together the company had been standing.

And then there was no sound at all.

Understand what that means. From the moment they had passed under the arch, the roots of that tree had been moving: turning, sliding, grinding one on another, across the better part of a mile of buried wood. They had been hearing it so long that they had stopped hearing it. When Kolate died, every root in the Forsaken Tree went still in the same instant, and the silence came down on the company like a hand. The driftglobe's light, which had burned red since the arch, paled toward its proper color. And in the hollow of the great root the little cage of rootlets, burned and regrown and burned again, uncurled like fingers and drew back, and left the coin lying in the open.

From the voice in the dark to the trident, I am told, the whole of it took about as long as it has taken you to read this page.

Nobody wanted to touch it. They remembered what had become of the last one who tried. But a company cannot stand looking at a coin forever, and it was Vex, who has never in his life let a silence go on too long, who walked up to it and gave it one cautious poke with a fingertip.

The red on the coin's face was not a color. It was spores, and they burst. A cloud of them sprayed from the hollow, and Dragonbait, who had come up close behind, took it full in the face. Shago, Urida and Vex himself caught the edges and coughed it out. The dragonborn retired several steps with what dignity he could assemble and proposed that perhaps someone better acquainted with magical devices might care to take the matter from there. He was looking at Urida.

It was the goblin who took it. Chumble dug an empty sack out of his pack, turned it inside out over his fist, and picked the Heart of Blight out of its hollow in the manner of a man tidying up after a dog. He folded the sack back over it and tied off the neck. An unseen hand, his own conjuring, patted him on the back throughout by way of encouragement. The coin did nothing further. It had spent its last defense on Vex.

As the heart left the root, the tree began to let go. All around them the roots sagged and crumbled, slowly, the way a sandbank goes, until what had been a living maze was only a warren of plain earthen tunnels. They needed no stone to find the way back. They walked out.

They had been below four hours, perhaps five. It was the middle of the afternoon when they came up into daylight, and the Obanashi were already celebrating. The arch no longer needed holding. The vines had fallen from it, and it stood open, and plainly would go on standing open. Artus Cimber sat on the ground beside it with his glove back on, grey in the face, having done nothing since morning but hold a door against a tree. Above them all the great trunk, which had been the black of old char when they went under it, was turning the dark grey-brown of ordinary bark even as they watched, and the brambles were thinning and dropping away. The tree was not dying. It had been dead for a hundred years, and it was stopping.

Xhosala came to them across the roots, and I am told she was nearer to weeping than a mage hunter likes to be seen. "Thank you. Thank you for what you have done. You have reclaimed it." And then, because she could not help herself: "Were you able to carry it out? Is it with you?"

It was with the goblin, and the goblin was off to one side with his back to everyone.

He had called Wickles back, and the pickle, restored and none the wiser, was being offered the sack. "Take it. Absorb its power," Chumble was heard to say. "You can do it. I believe in you." Wickles, I am assured, was only too happy to oblige. When holding it produced no visible result, the goblin opened the sack and stuffed the pickle in alongside the coin, and held it shut for a while. The Dill Titan did not emerge. A pickle emerged, and said it felt much the same as before.

Then Chumble Crudluck walked over and put the sack in Xhosala's hands, and her hands shook as she took it.

A hundred years the Obanashi have hunted that coin. They have buried more of their own beneath that tree than Xhosala cared to number for the company. This company went down after breakfast and brought it up before supper. I do not say it to diminish the Obanashi. I say it because I have been writing about these five for the better part of a year, and now and then one ought to step back from the banana in the dragonborn's pocket and observe what they have become.

Xhosala turned to Artus with the sack held against her chest. They would keep their word, she said. They would carry the heart back to the Seething Halls and set it in the portal where it had lain before Kolate ever came, and there were rites that would cleanse it, and when it was clean they would ask the spirits within it for whatever they held of Mezro. Mesika spoke up before Artus could. She would go with them. There was another clan that wanted the heart, as the company knew, and had spilled blood for it already, and she would see that it reached the temple.

Artus looked as though he wished to make the same offer and had to think how. He came over to the company instead. "It matters to me that they succeed in this," he said. "I know you are bound for Kir Sabal. Would you give me the time to go back to the temple with them, and meet you there a week behind?" It was five days to the Seething Halls and five days back to where they stood, he reasoned, and no call for the company to walk both. Let them go on to the aarakocra and learn what the bird-folk could offer toward the finding of Omu. "A view from the air, if they can give us one. I do not suppose they will carry us there themselves." He and Dragonbait would catch them up. Then he dropped his voice and added the other half of it: the journey would let him make certain that Mesika stayed at the temple when it was over.

The company told him to do as he liked. One of them remarked that, speaking for himself, he would not be sorry to have her along to Omu after all. She had, in fairness, shown what she could do with a rock.

It was then that the goblin asked for a word.

He asked for it in Goblin, and privately, by the little whispering cantrip he keeps for such occasions, and what he whispered was that he thought he had better go north with them. Artus did not mind. But Artus is not a fool, and he asked the obvious questions in the obvious order. Was he sure? What was going on? What had he done?

Well, said Chumble, he had given them the coin.

"What happened to Wickles, Chumble?"

"He may have absorbed whatever was inside it." And, after a moment: "They may be holding a plain, ordinary coin."

I set down the goblin's reasoning as he gave it. The pickle did not feel any different, not yet, but who could say how long that would last? He thought Wickles might need an exorcism. He would not send him away and call him back, as a warlock ordinarily may, for fear that sending him away would loose whatever was now inside him. He proposed to treat the matter as one treats a deadly poison, which travels faster through an excited body, and to that end he had fitted the pickle with a falconer's hood, the little leather cap that keeps a hawk quiet on the fist, and told him to sit still and think of nothing. And he had decided that the Obanashi should be left to believe they carried the Heart of the Wild. They would at least be careful with it.

I report this without comment, save one. I have no idea whether he is right. Neither has he. I print it in the confidence that the Obanashi are not among my subscribers.

So the company divided. North, toward the Seething Halls, went Xhosala and her five, with the sack; and Mesika, guarding it; and Artus Cimber and Dragonbait; and Chumble Crudluck, with a hooded pickle riding very still upon his shoulder. The parting was brief. "It has been real, and it has been fun," the goblin told Grieg, "but it has not been real fun." The wulven wished him joy of his pickle. "Do not die," he added. "I do not much care whether the pickle does." This was unkind, and I am told it landed, for Wickles had been warming to Grieg ever since the wulven stitched him a poncho some months back.

The rest made camp where they stood, in the shadow of a tree that was slowly remembering how to be one.

* * *

In the morning there were five of them: Grieg, Vex, Urida, Lilac Mist, and Shago to show the way. They turned southwest. Artus had told them of a gorge in that country, Ataaz Muhahah, the Laughing Gorge, and of the ancient span of stone that crosses it, which would save them days on the road to Kir Sabal.

They had not been walking long when Lilac Mist and Grieg both stopped and looked left. Thirty feet off the trail, under a hundred years of moss and creeper, something stood that the jungle had not made. They pulled the green away from it. It was a statue, ten feet tall: a king of Chult, done in the old stiff manner, crowned and robed for some high occasion and staring over their heads into the trees.

They asked Shago who it was, he being the only one among them born in this land. He studied it for a good while. He did not know. The merchant princes have ruled for as long as he can remember, he said, and this was none of them. Some leader out of the old days, important enough that somebody had cut his likeness in stone and carried it out here. As to its age, none of them is a mason, and the best they could make of the weathering was a hundred years at the least, give or take, as one of them put it, a hundred years.

Somebody had loved it once. There was tribute heaped at its feet, or the ghost of tribute: fruit long gone to black earth, the stems of flowers, things that had rotted to nothing in the rains. One offering had lasted. Lilac turned it up out of the mold, and Grieg, looking over her shoulder, knew it the moment she did.

Readers with long memories will know it too. It was an iron token, the twin of the one Shago carries in his own pouch: the mark he showed the company in Port Nyanzaru, when they brought him word that there was a writ out on his life, and that he called a mistake. This one was older, and pitted by the weather. It was otherwise the same in every particular, down to the lettering.

Nobody had ever read the lettering. Vex could. There is a gift that comes with the banner he fights under, and it lets him take the sense of any writing if he studies it long enough, whether or not he knows the tongue. He did not know this one. He turned the token in his fingers for some time, and then he read it out, feeling his way around a word that meant nothing to him.

"Ytepka Society."

He had never heard it. None of them had. Shago had never heard it spoken either. But he looked at the word for a long moment, and said slowly that it was an odd one, and that he thought he had seen it before, written down, on papers at home.

That is all they learned, and they are on a clock that does not allow for turning back to Port Nyanzaru to ask. So I leave it as they left it. A token that means death, laid as an offering at the feet of a forgotten king. I have my own notions about that word. They belong to a later chapter of this chronicle, and the reader shall have them there, when the company has earned theirs.

The day after was the fourth of Nightal, and nothing troubled it until dark. With the party so reduced they made new watches: Lilac Mist and Vex for the first, and Grieg, Shago and Urida for the second. The three lay down under the gnome's little dome. The two sat by a low fire, with the driftglobe dark so as to draw no eyes.

The first watch was hardly begun when something came running out of the jungle at them.

It was a tree. It was, to be exact, about a foot of tree: a sapling, up on its roots and running on them, rustling for all it was worth. It did not attack. It stopped short of the fire and shook itself at them, every leaf going, with the frantic purpose of a dog that needs you to follow it this instant.

Three days before, the company had gone down among roots that wanted them dead. Lilac Mist looked at this one and did the courteous thing. She greeted it in Sylvan, the tongue of the fey, and asked how they might help it.

The sapling stopped shaking. It tipped itself to one side, the way a dog cocks its head at a word it almost knows.

Then it answered her. It had not come for help. It had come with a warning, and it gave the warning and pointed, with what it had for an arm, into the dark to the south.

"You have undead coming your way."

VThe Horizon

The Road Ahead

What is known, and where they turn.
What Is Known
  • The Ring of Winter — on Artus Cimber's hand, gone north with the Heart and promised to Kir Sabal a week behind the company.
  • The Soulmonger — engine of the Death Curse, sunk in Omu between the Valley of Lost Honor and the Peaks of Flame.
  • The Death Curse — the dead will not stay raised, and the lately-raised waste in their flesh.
  • Azlund's Oath — fifty gold paid in hand, one rare gift each on return, if the Curse can be broken.
  • The Map of Chult — half-marked, given by Azlund at the Waterdeep docks.
  • The Sending Stone — Vex carries the pair, and Azlund waits at the other end.
  • The Yuan-ti's Door — a yuan-ti drew blood and fled within.
  • Saja N'baza — the naga atop Orolunga, who answered once and cannot be asked again.
  • The Heart of the Wild — pried from the Forsaken Tree once Kolate fell and handed to Xhosala for purifying, though Wickles may have swallowed whatever lived inside it.
Where They Turn

With the Heart carried out of the Forsaken Tree and Chumble gone north beside Artus, the rest of the company marches southwest for the bridge at Ataaz Muhahah on the way to Kir Sabal, and undead are closing in from the south.

Session I Sworn to Secrets, Bound for Chult — 1 Kythorn 15 MAR 2026

I have watched many a company assembled in the House of Healing, yet few so unlikely as this one. III. What Was in the Pack

That same night, the woman who called herself Nerissa could not sleep.

She lay a while in the hut among the others, and rose, and went out into the wet dark to breathe, the way people do who are running from something behind their own eyes. The dome closed behind her. And Lilac Mist and Urida, who were awake and on watch and had been listening to her breathe for an hour, looked at one another, and at the pack she had left beside her bedroll, and did not need to say anything at all.

I am a chronicler, not a magistrate. I record what was done. Lilac Mist went to the pack without a sound. Urida went with a wizard's thoroughness. The ranger searched. The gnome found.

There was, first, a ticket. A wager ticket, printed in the port, for the dinosaur races that run through Port Nyanzaru's streets every tenday to the ruin of a great many purses, mine among them on more than one occasion. A small thing. A human thing, and strange to find in the pack of a woman who claimed to have woken ten days ago in a flooded city a century out of time.

There was, second, a hidden sleeve sewn into the lining, and in it a journal.

Urida leafed through it fast, by the light that comes through a tiny hut's walls, which is to say by nearly none. It was about the Death Curse. That much she caught: pages of a hand that was not a warrior's hand, tracing the curse through the city. And as she turned the pages a loose sheet slid out of the back and fell into her lap, and she made a decision that I would have made and that most honest folk would not: she put the journal back exactly as it had been, and kept the letter.

It was addressed from someone called Wren to someone called Mesika.

A wax seal had once closed it, and the better part of the seal had crumbled away, but what remained showed a crescent moon, and beside it the left side of something curved, as though the moon were cradling the edge of a thing one could not quite make out. The hand inside was a sprawling, cheerful, slightly sideways hand, the hand of a man writing after dinner and some way into the evening. The cursed thingamawhatsit, Wren wrote, was not connected to the bleedin' curse that had been sucking all the fun out of the city. Who knew what it was really for; the society folk did not seem to know for certain, but thought those party-pooper yuan-ti might have been aiming at someone high up the chain. Either way, Kaya was still helping him forget as much of it as possible.

Three things happened to that letter in the next quarter hour, and each of them was done by a different member of the company, and I set them down because together they are as neat a piece of detective work as has ever been recounted to me outside a Waterdeep magistrate's hall.

Grieg, who had been asleep, was not any longer, and when the letter passed under his nose his nose did what a wulven's nose does. There was drink on the paper. Not the smell of a tavern, which clings to everything in Chult, but the particular ghost of good spirits spilled near a good pen. And Grieg, who has a hunter's memory for a scent once met, remembered a name he had heard in the port: Kaya, of Kaya's House of Repose, in the Market Ward. The letter had been written in an inn. He could tell them which.

Then they fetched Vex from his watch, because Vex is a noble of Waterdeep and a cousin to Azlund, and had spent his youth in rooms where certain quiet people came and went. He looked at the broken seal and knew it. The crescent moon with the harp's curve beside it: the mark of the Harpers, those spellcasters and spies who oppose the abuse of power everywhere in Faerûn and admit to it nowhere. He had seen it around his cousin's business more than once.

So, in the space of a watch, in a hut in the jungle: a Harper named Wren, writing from an inn in Port Nyanzaru, to a woman he called Mesika, about a cursed thing that was not the Death Curse and yuan-ti who had been aiming at someone important. And the woman who carried that letter in a hidden sleeve was outside in the rain telling herself she was Nerissa.

She came back in. She had heard something, a shuffle, a whisper, and she came in through the dome with a soldier's quickness and looked round at the company, and whatever she saw did not trouble her. She lay down. In a little while she slept, truly slept, the way the exhausted do.

And Lilac Mist went back to the pack.

This time she took the journal, and read it properly, with a ranger's patience, over the sleeping woman's own shoulder. The first entry was dated twenty-seven days before. The last was ten days old. It was the diary of an investigator: someone tracking the Death Curse through Port Nyanzaru with care and method, and with the dry, unhurried intelligence of a person who had done this kind of work before.

And it was wrong. Not in its facts: in its shape. There were places where the writer set down a discovery, a revelation, a thing learned and understood, and then, pages on and days later, set down the same discovery again, fresh, as if for the first time. Twice over. The hand was the same. The surprise was the same. Whoever wrote that journal had been learning the same things again and again, and each time believed it was the first.

Ten days ago, she had told them, the fire of every color came down on her. Ten days ago the journal stopped.

They put it back the next day, in secret, while the company marched: the journal to its hidden sleeve, the letter to its place among the later pages. She never knew it had been gone. I will leave it to my readers to decide whether that was kindness or merely good tactics. In this company it is often hard to tell the two apart, and I have stopped trying.

IV. The Gateway to Chult

Three more days of green. Three more nights of the dome and the watches and the forest's dripping clock. Then on the twenty-fourth of Uktar the trees fell away and the land went down northward to the sea, and there was Port Nyanzaru.

I have written of it before, and I will write of it again, because no city in the Realms repays the pen like this one. It climbs its hills in colors a Waterdhavian would call indecent: ochre and saffron and a blue the sky itself envies, roofs of red tile glittering with the last rain, the great domes of the Merchants' Ward catching the afternoon light. Beneath the colors the streets run with cart-dinosaurs and hawkers and the smell of frying plantain and the deeper, meatier smell that gives the Red Bazaar its name. Out in the bay, on its rock, the Temple of Sune stood with its mirrored walls making its roof seem to hang in the air, which is exactly the sort of thing Sune's people would do, and which I have always rather admired.

The company did what adventurers do in cities. They shopped, and what a company buys tells you what it expects. A shield for Chumble Crudluck. V. Kaya's House of Repose

A word about the house. Kaya's is one of the more prosperous inns of the Market Ward, built for travelers who want their comfort with a little gilt on it, and I give it four tankards out of five, docking one only because the house charges Waterdeep prices for Chultan rum and knows it. The common room is wide and lamplit, with a dartboard on the back wall that has seen more blood than some battlefields.

The man they wanted was not there when they arrived. He came later, when the lamps were lit and the room was full: a loose, friendly, red-faced fellow who sat with his back to the corner and a glass at his elbow and a handwritten book open on the table before him, and who gave off the air of a man who had been drunk in that chair so many evenings that the chair had begun to take his shape. Soggy Wren. The company did not approach him. They sent the pickle.

The company knew that roster. Then Grieg and Lilac Mist went to the dartboard.

They played badly, on purpose, which for a wulven hunter and a tabaxi ranger is harder than playing well, and on her turn Lilac Mist let a dart go wide, wide enough that it carried her across the room to retrieve it from the vicinity of the red-faced man's table. It was as unsubtle an approach as I have ever recorded, and it worked perfectly, because Soggy Wren is a man who has never in his life let a stranger pass within arm's length without offering them a drink. I will say that it was not gentle. They told him they had been hunting the curse since Waterdeep. They told him they had found Omu, the lost city under the canopy where the Soulmonger sits. They told him they meant to kill Ras Nsi. So he opened up. Drink makes some men liars and some men honest, and Wren is the second kind.

Chumble Crudluck raised a hand and shaped the air above the table, and a minor image stood there a moment in the lamplight: a tall dark woman with green eyes, in the gear of a city that no longer stood. Wren knew her before the goblin had finished. Mesika, he said: a good woman who had been helping him look into the curse.

Then he told them about the amulet. It had come into Port Nyanzaru in pieces, smuggled by the yuan-ti, and the Harpers and their friends in the Ytepka Society had gathered the pieces as they surfaced. Something the yuan-ti had made for a purpose of their own.

He put it on the table.

Urida laid her hands near it and spoke the words of identification, and the room went quiet around her the way rooms do around a wizard at work, and when she looked up she had it.

The amulet is a dreamer's tool. Set near a sleeping person, night after night, it works on them while they sleep, turning their sympathies by slow degrees toward the yuan-ti, until they wake one morning a friend to the serpent folk without ever having chosen to be. It does not work on just anyone. It is tuned, like a sending stone, to a single soul.

It was tuned to Jessamine.

Readers who have followed this chronicle will feel the floor shift as the company did. Jessamine, who sits among the seven merchant princes and casts the deciding vote when they are divided. Jessamine, who deals in plants and poison and sanctioned death, who hides her rotting flesh beneath black bandages because the Soulmonger's curse is eating her alive, and who, for exactly that reason, has twice now looked kindly on this company and their purpose. The yuan-ti had built a key for her sleeping mind. What they wanted with a merchant prince's vote, neither the company nor I can tell you; Wren's own letter had it that the amulet was no part of the Death Curse, and nothing the company has learned ties the serpent folk to it. But this is the same city where a yuan-ti pureblood was seen slipping away from a rooftop on the day a water barrel nearly killed Urida, and the company stopped taking the serpent folk's interest in Port Nyanzaru for coincidence a long time ago.

They had not managed it. The pieces had scattered, the Harpers had gathered them, and the thing now lay on a tavern table under a gnome's hands instead of under a merchant prince's pillow. But it had been meant, and in Chult what is meant once is generally meant again.

VI. What Remains

So the company came out of Kaya's House of Repose into the warm night with more than they went in with, as this company generally does.

They went in for a drunken Harper and came out with the name of the woman sleeping in Artus Cimber's camp: not Nerissa, but Mesika, warrior of Mezro, who a month ago was walking the streets of this city with a notebook and a wager ticket, investigating the Death Curse beside a man who could barely stand, and who ten days ago was struck by a fire of every color and woke believing she had just come from a patrol outside walls that fell a hundred years before. Her journal learns the same things over and over. Her mind is a dropped pot. And she is the last thread in all Chult that leads back to the lost city and the woman Artus has spent his life walking toward.

They mean to tell him. I would not care to be in the room.

Then they mean to go south again, back to the Halls between the rivers, to collect Xhosala from her reclaimed temple and go on south with her toward the Forsaken Tree, fifty miles further south between the Tiryki and the Olung, in blighted country where the dead walk in daylight: a darkwood trunk that rises above the canopy on the very spot where a priestess and a thief destroyed each other a century ago, and under whose roots a corrupted god's heart lies waiting in a coin.

They are better provisioned for it than they were. Eight potions. A ten-foot pole. A bag of ball bearings.

I have written of a great many heroes, and I have learned to distrust the ones who never turn back. This company turned back. They went home for potions and paid their guide and read a sleeping woman's diary and bought a drunk a drink, and in doing so they learned more in five days than most companies learn in a season. That is not the stuff of ballads. It is the stuff of victories.

Mark the name Mesika. And mark, if you are the marking kind, that the serpent folk wanted a merchant prince's dreams, and did not get them, and is unlikely to have stopped wanting.

My readers will recall the little gout of flame with which he closed his recital for the ghost Aviline, and which I called cosmetic. This one was not cosmetic. It took the pillar at its wounded middle and a man's height of root simply went to ash. The two ends dropped away from each other, and the black energy burst in the empty air between them and touched no one. Then the dragonborn walked out of the fog, unhurried, and took his place again at the saurial's side.

That was two. The third was the far one, scorched and thunder-struck and standing alone in the dark where only a goblin's eyes could find it. Chumble found it. One blast went wide. The second struck it square, and the pillar flew to splinters and spent its fury on an empty floor, unwitnessed, like the tree that falls in the forest in the old riddle.

That left one, the pillar on Kolate's own side of the chamber that had weathered the fireball. Grieg wanted it badly, and it was a long way round. He took the shortest line there was, close along the wall, where the lesser roots caught at his legs and tried to keep him, and he tore through them by main strength. He set his echo at the foot of the pillar and the short sword went to work again. It bit, and glanced, and Grieg dug for the burst of speed a fighter keeps in reserve for such moments, and it bit again and went through. The fourth pillar burst. The wulven lost a second shadow in as many minutes and did not appear to miss it.

With that the whole dark circuit that had so impressed the company from the doorway was broken. Nothing climbed the walls any more. Nothing poured into the heart. And there stood Kolate on the far side of the great root, with one of Shago's spears in him and nothing left at his back.

He did what he could. He turned the withering on Shago, and the guide set his jaw and took a small part of what was meant for him. Then it was Shago's turn. Kolate was quick: a shimmer of force sprang up on his arm and turned the first spear, and the second after it. It did not turn the shield. Shago put the rim of it into him with all his weight behind and laid him flat on his back among the roots, and across the chamber the yellow fog, with nobody left to mind it, thinned and went.

Urida came up on the other side and poured a cone of cold white light down the length of him, starlight by the look of it. It caught Shago's side as well, for he was standing over the man, and she called out that she was sorry, and Shago did not seem to notice he had been apologized to. Kolate noticed the light. When it faded he could see nothing farther off than the reach of a spear. And about his hands, where there had been nothing before, wisps of the black energy had begun to gather and turn.

They came at him from every quarter then. Mesika cut him once. Grieg stabbed down at him where he lay. Dragonbait was pounding around the root with the sword up. With every wound the darkness at his hands grew thicker.

I must leave them there a moment, because honesty requires me to report what Chumble Crudluck was doing while this went on.

He was at the coin. With the pillars down he had gone to the hollow in the great root to see what had killed his pickle, and found the heart shut in a little cage of rootlets, woven tight as a fist. So he took a flask of oil from his pack and emptied it over the cage, and struck a light from his tinderbox, and set it burning. The rootlets burned. They also grew, as fast as the fire could eat them, new wood pushing out through the flames. The goblin blew on it. That not sufficing, he turned his back on it and shook out the cloak the captain of the Horizon gave this company on their very first voyage, which billows grandly on command and had never until that hour been asked to do anything useful, and he fanned the fire with it. The fire roared. The roots grew. I am told it was a draw.

Meanwhile Kolate, flat on his back and all but blind, spoke a word and was not there.

Swords came down on bare roots. For a moment nobody knew where he had gone, save the two who could see the other side of the chamber. But a wulven's ears are not a man's. Grieg heard him land: a scuffle, and a weight coming to its feet against the far wall, beyond the great root. He shouted it out, and the company turned like a pack on a new scent.

Shago was off before the shout had finished, into a dark he could not see through, because that is the manner of the man. His road took him around the heart, past a goblin with his back to a fire, flapping a cloak. Chumble put out a hand for a slap of the palm as he went by. I cannot tell you whether he got one. I can tell you that Shago would have tried.

Urida's thunder found Kolate first, and he bore it. Then Lilac Mist had him. The fight had come round to her side of the chamber at last, and she laid her mark on him and loosed twice. The first arrow missed. The second he raised his shimmer against, and it went through that and through him as though neither were there. The darkness around his hands was by now blacker than anything else in that black place.

And Mesika, who had come down into the Forsaken Tree with a khopesh on each hip and no bow, and who found herself fifty feet from the enemy of her new friends with nothing to throw, did the thing a soldier does. She picked up rocks. The first two went nowhere near him. The third hit him square on the top of the head.

Chumble let the fire alone long enough to throw a larger one. It was the last great spell he had in him that day, and Kolate twisted away from the worst of it, and came out of the flames scorched and staggering and still gathering the dark to himself.

Every eye in the company could see what was building in his hands. None of them knew its name. They all knew they had come running to one place to kill him, and were now standing shoulder to shoulder around a man with one great spell left.

Grieg ran. He covered half the distance on his own legs and threw his echo the rest, and from where it stood over Kolate he struck through it with both hands on the trident. The first thrust went home. The necromancer sat up out of the roots with his hands coming together, the black energy pouring between them into a shape that was round and wide and meant for all of them. The second thrust went through both hands at once and into what lay behind them.

Urida never recognized the spell. I have described the gathering of it to others who know more of such workings than I do, and they went quiet, and asked how close together the company had been standing.

And then there was no sound at all.

Understand what that means. From the moment they had passed under the arch, the roots of that tree had been moving: turning, sliding, grinding one on another, across the better part of a mile of buried wood. They had been hearing it so long that they had stopped hearing it. When Kolate died, every root in the Forsaken Tree went still in the same instant, and the silence came down on the company like a hand. The driftglobe's light, which had burned red since the arch, paled toward its proper color. And in the hollow of the great root the little cage of rootlets, burned and regrown and burned again, uncurled like fingers and drew back, and left the coin lying in the open.

From the voice in the dark to the trident, I am told, the whole of it took about as long as it has taken you to read this page.

Nobody wanted to touch it. They remembered what had become of the last one who tried. But a company cannot stand looking at a coin forever, and it was Vex, who has never in his life let a silence go on too long, who walked up to it and gave it one cautious poke with a fingertip.

The red on the coin's face was not a color. It was spores, and they burst. A cloud of them sprayed from the hollow, and Dragonbait, who had come up close behind, took it full in the face. Shago, Urida and Vex himself caught the edges and coughed it out. The dragonborn retired several steps with what dignity he could assemble and proposed that perhaps someone better acquainted with magical devices might care to take the matter from there. He was looking at Urida.

It was the goblin who took it. Chumble dug an empty sack out of his pack, turned it inside out over his fist, and picked the Heart of Blight out of its hollow in the manner of a man tidying up after a dog. He folded the sack back over it and tied off the neck. An unseen hand, his own conjuring, patted him on the back throughout by way of encouragement. The coin did nothing further. It had spent its last defense on Vex.

As the heart left the root, the tree began to let go. All around them the roots sagged and crumbled, slowly, the way a sandbank goes, until what had been a living maze was only a warren of plain earthen tunnels. They needed no stone to find the way back. They walked out.

They had been below four hours, perhaps five. It was the middle of the afternoon when they came up into daylight, and the Obanashi were already celebrating. The arch no longer needed holding. The vines had fallen from it, and it stood open, and plainly would go on standing open. Artus Cimber sat on the ground beside it with his glove back on, grey in the face, having done nothing since morning but hold a door against a tree. Above them all the great trunk, which had been the black of old char when they went under it, was turning the dark grey-brown of ordinary bark even as they watched, and the brambles were thinning and dropping away. The tree was not dying. It had been dead for a hundred years, and it was stopping.

Xhosala came to them across the roots, and I am told she was nearer to weeping than a mage hunter likes to be seen. "Thank you. Thank you for what you have done. You have reclaimed it." And then, because she could not help herself: "Were you able to carry it out? Is it with you?"

It was with the goblin, and the goblin was off to one side with his back to everyone.

He had called Wickles back, and the pickle, restored and none the wiser, was being offered the sack. "Take it. Absorb its power," Chumble was heard to say. "You can do it. I believe in you." Wickles, I am assured, was only too happy to oblige. When holding it produced no visible result, the goblin opened the sack and stuffed the pickle in alongside the coin, and held it shut for a while. The Dill Titan did not emerge. A pickle emerged, and said it felt much the same as before.

Then Chumble Crudluck walked over and put the sack in Xhosala's hands, and her hands shook as she took it.

A hundred years the Obanashi have hunted that coin. They have buried more of their own beneath that tree than Xhosala cared to number for the company. This company went down after breakfast and brought it up before supper. I do not say it to diminish the Obanashi. I say it because I have been writing about these five for the better part of a year, and now and then one ought to step back from the banana in the dragonborn's pocket and observe what they have become.

Xhosala turned to Artus with the sack held against her chest. They would keep their word, she said. They would carry the heart back to the Seething Halls and set it in the portal where it had lain before Kolate ever came, and there were rites that would cleanse it, and when it was clean they would ask the spirits within it for whatever they held of Mezro. Mesika spoke up before Artus could. She would go with them. There was another clan that wanted the heart, as the company knew, and had spilled blood for it already, and she would see that it reached the temple.

Artus looked as though he wished to make the same offer and had to think how. He came over to the company instead. "It matters to me that they succeed in this," he said. "I know you are bound for Kir Sabal. Would you give me the time to go back to the temple with them, and meet you there a week behind?" It was five days to the Seething Halls and five days back to where they stood, he reasoned, and no call for the company to walk both. Let them go on to the aarakocra and learn what the bird-folk could offer toward the finding of Omu. "A view from the air, if they can give us one. I do not suppose they will carry us there themselves." He and Dragonbait would catch them up. Then he dropped his voice and added the other half of it: the journey would let him make certain that Mesika stayed at the temple when it was over.

The company told him to do as he liked. One of them remarked that, speaking for himself, he would not be sorry to have her along to Omu after all. She had, in fairness, shown what she could do with a rock.

It was then that the goblin asked for a word.

He asked for it in Goblin, and privately, by the little whispering cantrip he keeps for such occasions, and what he whispered was that he thought he had better go north with them. Artus did not mind. But Artus is not a fool, and he asked the obvious questions in the obvious order. Was he sure? What was going on? What had he done?

Well, said Chumble, he had given them the coin.

"What happened to Wickles, Chumble?"

"He may have absorbed whatever was inside it." And, after a moment: "They may be holding a plain, ordinary coin."

I set down the goblin's reasoning as he gave it. The pickle did not feel any different, not yet, but who could say how long that would last? He thought Wickles might need an exorcism. He would not send him away and call him back, as a warlock ordinarily may, for fear that sending him away would loose whatever was now inside him. He proposed to treat the matter as one treats a deadly poison, which travels faster through an excited body, and to that end he had fitted the pickle with a falconer's hood, the little leather cap that keeps a hawk quiet on the fist, and told him to sit still and think of nothing. And he had decided that the Obanashi should be left to believe they carried the Heart of the Wild. They would at least be careful with it.

I report this without comment, save one. I have no idea whether he is right. Neither has he. I print it in the confidence that the Obanashi are not among my subscribers.

So the company divided. North, toward the Seething Halls, went Xhosala and her five, with the sack; and Mesika, guarding it; and Artus Cimber and Dragonbait; and Chumble Crudluck, with a hooded pickle riding very still upon his shoulder. The parting was brief. "It has been real, and it has been fun," the goblin told Grieg, "but it has not been real fun." The wulven wished him joy of his pickle. "Do not die," he added. "I do not much care whether the pickle does." This was unkind, and I am told it landed, for Wickles had been warming to Grieg ever since the wulven stitched him a poncho some months back.

The rest made camp where they stood, in the shadow of a tree that was slowly remembering how to be one.

* * *

In the morning there were five of them: Grieg, Vex, Urida, Lilac Mist, and Shago to show the way. They turned southwest. Artus had told them of a gorge in that country, Ataaz Muhahah, the Laughing Gorge, and of the ancient span of stone that crosses it, which would save them days on the road to Kir Sabal.

They had not been walking long when Lilac Mist and Grieg both stopped and looked left. Thirty feet off the trail, under a hundred years of moss and creeper, something stood that the jungle had not made. They pulled the green away from it. It was a statue, ten feet tall: a king of Chult, done in the old stiff manner, crowned and robed for some high occasion and staring over their heads into the trees.

They asked Shago who it was, he being the only one among them born in this land. He studied it for a good while. He did not know. The merchant princes have ruled for as long as he can remember, he said, and this was none of them. Some leader out of the old days, important enough that somebody had cut his likeness in stone and carried it out here. As to its age, none of them is a mason, and the best they could make of the weathering was a hundred years at the least, give or take, as one of them put it, a hundred years.

Somebody had loved it once. There was tribute heaped at its feet, or the ghost of tribute: fruit long gone to black earth, the stems of flowers, things that had rotted to nothing in the rains. One offering had lasted. Lilac turned it up out of the mold, and Grieg, looking over her shoulder, knew it the moment she did.

Readers with long memories will know it too. It was an iron token, the twin of the one Shago carries in his own pouch: the mark he showed the company in Port Nyanzaru, when they brought him word that there was a writ out on his life, and that he called a mistake. This one was older, and pitted by the weather. It was otherwise the same in every particular, down to the lettering.

Nobody had ever read the lettering. Vex could. There is a gift that comes with the banner he fights under, and it lets him take the sense of any writing if he studies it long enough, whether or not he knows the tongue. He did not know this one. He turned the token in his fingers for some time, and then he read it out, feeling his way around a word that meant nothing to him.

"Ytepka Society."

He had never heard it. None of them had. Shago had never heard it spoken either. But he looked at the word for a long moment, and said slowly that it was an odd one, and that he thought he had seen it before, written down, on papers at home.

That is all they learned, and they are on a clock that does not allow for turning back to Port Nyanzaru to ask. So I leave it as they left it. A token that means death, laid as an offering at the feet of a forgotten king. I have my own notions about that word. They belong to a later chapter of this chronicle, and the reader shall have them there, when the company has earned theirs.

The day after was the fourth of Nightal, and nothing troubled it until dark. With the party so reduced they made new watches: Lilac Mist and Vex for the first, and Grieg, Shago and Urida for the second. The three lay down under the gnome's little dome. The two sat by a low fire, with the driftglobe dark so as to draw no eyes.

The first watch was hardly begun when something came running out of the jungle at them.

It was a tree. It was, to be exact, about a foot of tree: a sapling, up on its roots and running on them, rustling for all it was worth. It did not attack. It stopped short of the fire and shook itself at them, every leaf going, with the frantic purpose of a dog that needs you to follow it this instant.

Three days before, the company had gone down among roots that wanted them dead. Lilac Mist looked at this one and did the courteous thing. She greeted it in Sylvan, the tongue of the fey, and asked how they might help it.

The sapling stopped shaking. It tipped itself to one side, the way a dog cocks its head at a word it almost knows.

Then it answered her. It had not come for help. It had come with a warning, and it gave the warning and pointed, with what it had for an arm, into the dark to the south.

"You have undead coming your way."
